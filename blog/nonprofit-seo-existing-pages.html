Before you write more content, check the pages people already find

By Ismael Melendez

A useful starting point for improving a nonprofit website is to look at pages that already appear in search. People may be finding them, but the page title, content, or next step may not be helping those visitors.

In a nonprofit SEO project, I used Google Search Console and other analytics tools to identify search topics with substantial visibility but relatively few clicks. I worked with a web development colleague on changes to page addresses and headings, and on restoring useful pages lost during an earlier redesign.

What the search numbers could tell us

We followed five search topics over several months. The project summary records increases in clicks and impressions across those topics, with mixed results for click-through rate.

Those measures answer different questions. Impressions describe appearances in search results. Clicks describe visits initiated from those results. Click-through rate describes how often an appearance resulted in a click.

An increase in visibility did not automatically mean every measure improved. It also did not establish how many people completed a membership signup. Tracking that next step was part of the recommended follow-up work.

Why that distinction matters

A report can show encouraging search activity while leaving the organization's main question unanswered: did people find what they needed and take a useful next step?

My role included identifying opportunities, collaborating on changes, monitoring results, and documenting recommendations. The project was collaborative, and the available summary does not establish that a single change caused the observed increases.

What I would check on a small team's website

Start with a page that supports an important service or resource. Read its search title and opening paragraph as someone unfamiliar with the organization. Check that the page is available, answers the question it promises to answer, and links to a relevant next step.

If a redesign changed its address, check whether the old link still reaches the right content. Refresh dates when the content is meaningfully reviewed or changed; an automatically changing date is not evidence that information is current.

For measurement today, I would pair Search Console's search-performance view with GA4's view of activity on the site. That is a current recommendation, not a claim that GA4 was used in this historical project.

A practical next step

Choose one important page before commissioning a new batch of articles. Record the problem, make a focused improvement, and review the evidence over time. Account for changes in demand and other work happening on the site.
